## What the buyer usually means

On a real call, “it’s too expensive” usually means one of four things:

- **“I don’t see the value yet.”** You’ve put the price on the table, but not the payoff.
- **“I have a cheaper quote.”** They’re comparing, often apples to oranges.
- **“This doesn’t fit my budget.”** A real limit, but often a question of scope or timing.
- **“I want to negotiate.”** They’re testing whether your price holds.

You can’t respond well until you know which one you’re dealing with. That’s why your first move is almost always a question, not an answer.

## Three price objection responses you can use on the call

### 1. Ask what they’re comparing you to

> “Too expensive compared to what? Are you looking at another option, or is it more about budget?”

It’s neutral, it doesn’t sound defensive, and the answer tells you which of the four cases you’re in.

### 2. Do the ROI math with their numbers

> “Let’s run the numbers together. You mentioned your team loses about four hours a week to double entry. Across 20 reps, that’s 80 hours a week. What’s an hour of their time worth to you?”

Use the buyer’s numbers, not yours. People trust math built on figures they gave you themselves.

### 3. Change the scope, not the price

> “If budget is the issue, we could start with the team where the impact is biggest and expand from there. Which team would that be?”

Your per-seat price stays intact, and the buyer still has a path to yes.

If you’ve been trained on Feel-Felt-Found (“I understand how you feel, others felt the same way, and here’s what they found”), it works best after that first question. Use it too early and it sounds scripted, because you don’t know yet what the buyer is actually worried about.

## The mistake most reps make

Offering a discount right away. When a discount is your first move, the buyer hears that your price was never serious. It lowers the perceived value and invites the next ask. Concessions belong at the end of a negotiation, never at the start.
